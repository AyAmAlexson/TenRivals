## Blog Post 1

Slug: `5-rules-for-choosing-your-first-real-tennis-racket`

Title: `5 Essential Rules for Choosing Your First Real Racket`

Lead:

Moving from a rental racket to your own is a milestone. Here is how to pick a frame that grows with you, not against you.

Body block 1:

Walking into a shop and seeing a full wall of rackets can feel overwhelming. Most beginners make the same mistake: they look at what a tour player uses and assume that is the right answer for them. In reality, the best first racket is not the most demanding one. It is the frame that helps you build clean contact, stay relaxed through long sessions, and trust your swing under pressure.

Rule number one: bigger is usually better. For most beginners, a head size between 100 and 105 square inches is the sweet spot. A slightly larger head gives you a more forgiving response and a bigger effective sweet spot, which means fewer mishits and more confidence as your technique develops.

Rule number two: respect the 300 gram limit. For most recreational players in Tbilisi, a racket in the 280 to 300 gram range unstrung gives the best balance of stability and comfort. Go too heavy and your arm gets tired by the second set. Go too light and the frame can feel unstable against a heavier ball.

Quote:

`The best racket for you is not the one Roger Federer uses. It is the one that makes you feel calm, clean, and confident on a windy day at Mziuri.`

Quote author:

`Andrey, Founder of Tennis Rivals`

Body block 2:

Rule number three: graphite is non-negotiable. If you are serious enough to buy your first real frame, skip alloy and generic composition rackets. A full graphite construction gives you a cleaner feel at contact, better vibration dampening, and a healthier long-term experience for your wrist and elbow.

Rule number four: do not guess your grip size. For many men in Georgia, Grip 3 is the default starting point, and for many women Grip 2 is the safer place to begin. If you are between sizes, go smaller. You can always build the handle up with an overgrip, but making a grip smaller is far harder.

## Blog Post 2

Slug: `why-tennis-shoes-matter-hard-vs-clay-guide`

Title: `Why Running Shoes Are a Risk on the Tennis Court`

Lead:

Your movement is the foundation of your game. If you are playing in running shoes, you are not just hurting your tennis. You are also increasing your risk of injury.

Body block 1:

Tennis is built on sudden stops, recovery steps, lateral pushes, and awkward defensive positions. Running shoes are designed for forward motion, soft landings, and repetitive straight-line movement. That mismatch matters far more than most players realize.

The first difference is lateral stability. Tennis shoes are built with stronger sidewalls and more secure upper construction to help stop the foot from rolling over during wide movement. Running shoes usually sit softer and higher, which can feel comfortable for jogging but unstable when you are reaching hard to the forehand corner.

The second difference is outsole logic. In Tbilisi, many players move between hard courts and clay depending on season, club, and session type. On hard courts, you need durability and controlled grip. On clay, you need a pattern that can bite the surface without turning your movement into panic braking.

Quote:

`I have seen more problems from the wrong shoes than from the wrong racket. On a hard court, your shoes are your first piece of safety equipment.`

Quote author:

`Andrey, Founder of Tennis Rivals`

Body block 2:

Hard court shoes are usually designed around durability because the surface eats rubber quickly. Clay shoes use deeper and more consistent herringbone-style patterns to manage dust, improve traction, and support controlled sliding.

Then there is toe durability. If you serve correctly or slide into open-stance shots, you will often drag the back foot. Proper tennis shoes include reinforced zones that protect the upper from wearing through too quickly. Standard running shoes simply are not built for that pattern of stress.

## Blog Post 3

Slug: `the-truth-about-tennis-strings-restringing-guide`

Title: `The Racket Is the Body, but the Strings Are the Soul`

Lead:

Up to half of your racket’s performance comes from the string bed. If you have not restrung in months, you are not really playing with the same racket anymore.

Body block 1:

Most players are willing to spend serious money on a racket and then ignore the strings until they snap. That is one of the biggest performance mistakes in amateur tennis. Strings start losing tension from the moment the racket comes off the machine, and once that process continues too far, the feel changes completely.

Dead strings lose elasticity, pocketing, and touch. The ball starts launching unpredictably, comfort drops, and the racket feels harsher even though nothing about the frame itself has changed.

Quote:

`Do not wait for your strings to break before you change them. If the ball is flying long for no reason or you feel like you lost touch, fresh strings are probably the answer.`

Quote author:

`Andrey, Founder of Tennis Rivals`

Body block 2:

A simple rule works well for 2.5 to 3.5 players: restring as many times per year as you play per week. If you play twice a week, treat every six months as a hard maximum rather than a suggestion.

Material choice matters too. Multifilament strings are softer, more powerful, and usually friendlier on the arm. Polyester strings reward bigger swings with more spin and control, but they make more sense when you have enough racket head speed to activate them properly.

Tension also matters more than most players think. Lower tension can make the sweet spot feel more forgiving and improve comfort without turning the racket into a trampoline. For many club players in Tbilisi, a slightly softer setup creates a better real-world result than chasing a stiff, tour-style response.

This is where a smart string setup becomes a real upgrade. The right string, the right tension, and the right grip feel can make a racket feel alive again.

If you want more spin, better touch, or simply a cleaner sense of control, start by looking at your strings before blaming your racket. A fresh setup often changes the whole experience of the frame.
